## Quiz: Prioritisation Checkpoint

Comes after the lesson: **Prioritisation Checkpoint**

### Question 1

A weighted-scoring session is half done. The scores are on the screen. The sales director says, “Strategic fit matters more than we thought. Let's move it from 15% to 35% and take the difference from customer impact.” His deal-backed item jumps from fifth to first.

**What is the right response?**

A. Accept the change, because weighted scoring is transparent and everyone can see the new weights
B. Keep the weights agreed before scoring for this round. If strategy really matters more, agree and date the new weights before the next scoring starts  ✅ correct
C. Average the rankings from the old and the new weights, so both views are represented
D. Drop weighted scoring and use RICE, because RICE has no weights to argue about

*Explanation:* Weights changed after the scores are visible give you a ranking chosen first and justified second. Nothing about the items changed, only the weights. The protection is procedural: criteria and weights agreed, written down and dated before anyone scores. Averaging two rankings blesses the change by half. Switching to RICE doesn't remove the problem: RICE's impact anchor and confidence can be bent in the same way.

*Lesson it draws on:* Value vs Effort, ICE and Weighted Scoring

### Question 2

You are scoring a bulk-export feature. Reach comes from usage data on the current export. Engineering has given an estimate for effort. The impact figure is borrowed from a competitor's case study about their own export tool.

**Using the evidence rule from Intercom's own examples, what confidence should it get?**

A. 100%, because two of the three estimates have hard evidence behind them
B. 50%, because anything borrowed from a competitor means every estimate could be off
C. 80%: data for reach and effort, but you are unsure about impact  ✅ correct
D. Below 50%, because a competitor's case study makes it a moonshot

*Explanation:* The rule: 100% means data for reach, research for impact and an engineering estimate for effort; 80% means data for reach and effort but unsure about impact; 50% means reach, impact and effort could all be off. Here reach and effort are backed, and impact isn't, so 80%. 100% would need research behind the impact. 50% and below would understate the evidence you do have, which is the downward version of the same fudge.

*Lesson it draws on:* RICE, Done Honestly

### Question 3

A release has 20 person-weeks of capacity: Musts 11, Shoulds 5, Coulds 4. Halfway through, one Must turns out to need 3 more person-weeks. The release date is fixed.

**What does MoSCoW, used as designed, tell you to do?**

A. Drop Coulds to protect the Musts, so the release still lands on its date  ✅ correct
B. Move the release date, because every Must has to ship
C. Re-label the overrunning Must as a Should, so the release stays on plan
D. Drop a Should first, because Shoulds are the biggest block of non-Must work

*Explanation:* The Musts were at 55%, under the roughly 60% rule, precisely so that an overrun can be absorbed by dropping Coulds. Coulds are the contingency and the first thing to go. Moving the date breaks the fixed timebox MoSCoW is there to protect. Re-labelling a Must because it got expensive ignores the four tests, which ask whether the release works without it, not what it costs. Shoulds are painful to drop and come after the Coulds.

*Lesson it draws on:* MoSCoW for a Fixed Timebox

### Question 4

One team, three items. A: about $10k a week of cost of delay, 2 weeks of work. B: about $24k a week, 8 weeks. C: a regulatory change that takes 6 weeks and must be live by week 10. Its cost of delay today is zero.

**What goes on the plan first?**

A. B first, because it has the highest weekly cost of delay
B. A, then B, then C, which is the CD3 order
C. C last, because CD3 scores it at zero and the date is still weeks away
D. Pin C's last safe start, week 4 at the latest and earlier with a buffer, then order A before B around it by CD3  ✅ correct

*Explanation:* CD3 gives A 10 ÷ 2 = 5 and B 24 ÷ 8 = 3, so A comes before B. But C has an external deadline, so its cost of delay is zero until the last week you can start, then it jumps. Ten weeks minus six weeks of work means it must start by week 4. So you schedule date-bound work backwards from its date first, then let CD3 order everything else. Ranking by weekly cost alone ignores duration, and trusting CD3 for C is how you miss the date.

*Lesson it draws on:* Cost of Delay and Urgency Profiles

### Question 5

In the quarterly review, the CEO says: “RICE ranks my idea second and WSJF ranks it sixth. Your own models disagree, so let's use RICE and build it.” The idea is untested, sits at 50% confidence, and its cost of delay range runs from zero to a large number.

**What is your best answer?**

A. Agree, because RICE is the more rigorous model and includes a confidence input
B. Explain that RICE leads with value against effort and WSJF with the weeks. Then show the inputs: 50% confidence, a wide cost-of-delay range, and a cheap test with a pass line and a date, plus what the full build would push out  ✅ correct
C. Average the two ranks, which puts the idea fourth, and fund it from there
D. Defend the WSJF ranking, because time criticality makes it the more accurate model

*Explanation:* The models disagree because they put different lines of the bill on top: value for RICE, the weeks for WSJF. You don't pick the model whose answer you like. You move the argument onto inputs the CEO can change, offer the test that would change them, and name the price of the full build. Averaging ranks hides what each model can see. Defending either score gives her nothing to change except the ranking, which is how frameworks get overridden.

*Lesson it draws on:* When the Models Disagree, and the Call
