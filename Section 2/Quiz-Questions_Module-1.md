## Quiz: Lifecycle Checkpoint

Comes after the lesson: **Lifecycle Checkpoint**

### Question 1

On Tuesday you build a working payment-link prototype with an AI prototyping tool. Six of eight customers you show it to say they would use it. Your VP says: “Great, ship that prototype to everyone next week.”

**What is the most accurate reply?**

A. “Agreed. Customers have validated it, and that was the hard part.”
B. “The prototype has reduced value and usability risk. It has not shown scale, security, privacy or reliability, and that is what delivery builds.”  ✅ correct
C. “We should keep testing until all eight customers say yes before we build anything.”
D. “Prototype feedback cannot tell us anything about value, so we should ignore it.”

*Explanation:* Cagan's line (SVPG, Apr 2026) is that discovery is building to learn and delivery is building to earn. A cheap prototype is excellent evidence about whether people want something and can use it. It says little about scale, security, privacy or reliability. Waiting for eight out of eight is a false bar, and prototypes are exactly how teams test value.

*Lesson it draws on:* The PDLC Is a Pattern

### Question 2

A finance director reads a lifecycle chart in an offsite deck and says: “Core invoicing is in maturity, so decline comes next. Let's start winding down investment now.”

**What is the strongest response?**

A. Agree. The curve is a reliable forecast, so decline is the next step
B. Argue that core invoicing is still in growth, so investment should go up
C. Point out that the lifecycle stopped applying to software products years ago
D. Treat the stage as a lens, not a forecast: stages are clearest in hindsight, and Levitt argued life can be extended with new uses and customers  ✅ correct

*Explanation:* Levitt popularised the product lifecycle in 1965 and argued that new uses and new customers can extend a product's life. Maturity changes the question to what to protect and where effort pays back. It does not mean decline is due. Arguing about which stage you are in misses the point, and calling the model dead is not supported either.

*Lesson it draws on:* The PDLC Is a Pattern

### Question 3

Retro on instant payouts. Customers loved it, it was one tap to use, engineering shipped on schedule, and it was paused a quarter later because every payout cost more in partner fees than Tallyrow charged.

**In Cagan's model, whose risk was that?**

A. The product manager's: business viability is one of the two risks the PM owns  ✅ correct
B. The finance lead's, because the problem showed up in a partner invoice
C. The lead engineer's, because the feature was built on the partner's service
D. The designer's, because a better pricing screen would have prevented it

*Explanation:* Cagan (SVPG, 2017) gives the PM value and business viability, the designer usability, and the lead engineer feasibility. Finance found the problem but did not own the risk; the cheap check would have been one question to finance before building. Feasibility cleared, because the feature was built and shipped. Usability cleared too.

*Lesson it draws on:* The Four Risks

### Question 4

Maya, the CEO, posts in the planning channel: “Can we just RICE everything and see what comes out on top?” Smart reminders, her favourite, has never been tested with a customer.

**What is the best reply?**

A. Score everything, and give smart reminders 100% confidence because the CEO backs it
B. Refuse to rank anything until every backlog item has been tested with customers
C. Run a Kano survey on all eight items instead, because Kano is more customer-centric than RICE
D. Rank the backlog, but send smart reminders to discovery first with one cheap test of whether customers want them  ✅ correct

*Explanation:* Name the question first, then pick the model. RICE answers “which gives the most expected impact for the effort”; the open question about smart reminders is whether customers want them at all, which is a discovery question. Setting confidence to 100% because the CEO backs the idea is exactly how RICE gets gamed. Blocking all ranking, or swapping in one different model for everything, repeats the same mistake from the other side.

*Lesson it draws on:* The Decision Map

### Question 5

A reach-based score puts e-invoicing near the bottom, because only one market is affected. Aisha, the compliance lead, escalates: the legal deadline in that market is fixed.

**What is the right next move?**

A. Raise e-invoicing's Impact score to 3 so that it climbs the ranking
B. Recognise that a fixed date is an urgency question the reach-based score does not measure, and size it with Cost of Delay  ✅ correct
C. Keep the ranking, because changing it after an escalation undermines the framework
D. Mark e-invoicing a Must and everything else a Won't

*Explanation:* A reach-based score can undervalue a fixed-date item, because it does not measure urgency. Cost of Delay does, and its external-deadline profile is built for this kind of item. Inflating Impact to win the argument is gaming the score. Holding the ranking ignores real information. Making everything else a Won't misuses MoSCoW, which scopes a timebox rather than ranking a backlog.

*Lesson it draws on:* The Decision Map
