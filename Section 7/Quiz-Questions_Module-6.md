# Product Management: Prioritisation, Metrics & A/B Testing

5 multiple-choice questions. The correct answer is marked ✅; the explanation is shown after the learner answers.

## Quiz: Agile Cadence Checkpoint

Comes after the lesson: **Agile Cadence Checkpoint**

### Question 1

Your new team's backlog has 60 items, each tagged High, Medium or Low. Thirty-one are High. The engineering lead asks you, as Product Owner, “Which High do we pull first?”

**What should you change?**

A. Re-tag the backlog so that only ten items are High
B. Let the engineers pick from the Highs, since they know the effort
C. Add a Critical tag above High for the most urgent items
D. Turn the labels into an order, one item first and then the next: the quarter's call sets what's in, a value model orders the work inside it, and fixed dates are points the order must respect  ✅ correct

*Explanation:* The Scrum Guide makes the Product Owner accountable for ordering the backlog: one thing first, then the next. Labels hide the trade-off, and fewer Highs or a new Critical tag is still a label. The models feed the order. Handing the choice to whoever pulls the ticket means the ranking quietly dies in sprint planning.

*Lesson it draws on:* From Ranked Backlog to Sprint

### Question 2

Three drafts of a sprint goal for a squad working on invoice disputes.

**Which is the best sprint goal?**

A. A business can resolve a disputed invoice with its client without contacting support  ✅ correct
B. Close tickets DSP-101 to DSP-118
C. Deliver 34 story points
D. Improve the disputes experience

*Explanation:* The Scrum Guide calls the Sprint Goal the single objective for the sprint. A good one is a slice of an outcome that a ticket either serves or doesn't. A ticket list and a points total describe the work, not why it matters. “Improve the disputes experience” is too vague to tell you which tickets can wait.

*Lesson it draws on:* From Ranked Backlog to Sprint

### Question 3

After a heated meeting, the head of sales asks you to move his integration from eighth to second. No new data, customers or deals have come in since planning. He says, “It's obviously more important than you've scored it.”

**What is the best response?**

A. Move it to second, because a senior stakeholder's judgement counts as evidence
B. Refuse and lock the ranking until next quarter, so it stays stable
C. Ask what evidence would change its inputs, such as a deal with a date or customer counts. Change the order when that evidence arrives, and write the reason next to the change  ✅ correct
D. Re-score the whole backlog this week, so the change doesn't look singled out

*Explanation:* Change the order when the evidence changes, not when the volume does. A ranking with a written history is one you can defend, and a silent change looks like politics. Locking the ranking is the other failure: rankings die when nobody updates the inputs. Re-scoring everything because one person pushed turns the backlog into weather.

*Lesson it draws on:* From Ranked Backlog to Sprint

### Question 4

The board asks for a 12-month roadmap with a ship date on every item. You have one fixed regulatory date, two items being shaped, and several problems you haven't chosen solutions for yet.

**What do you present?**

A. A full timeline with a date on every item, because that is what the board asked for
B. A Now, Next, Later roadmap with no dates at all, because dates promise certainty nobody has
C. A timeline with dates, plus a note saying all dates may change
D. A Now, Next, Later roadmap, with rows written as outcomes, a real date where it exists (the regulatory one), and ranges where it doesn't  ✅ correct

*Explanation:* Most timelines promise a certainty nobody has. Now, Next, Later shows what is committed, what is being shaped, and what is still a problem rather than a solution. Leaders who need dates will push back, so give dates where they're real and ranges where they aren't. Removing every date ignores the real one. A disclaimer under made-up dates doesn't make them true.

*Lesson it draws on:* Quarterly Planning at Scale

### Question 5

Your weekly metrics review has run for ten weeks. Every chart is presented in turn, it takes an hour, and it has never changed a decision. Attendance is dropping.

**What is the best fix?**

A. Move it to monthly, so there's more to discuss each time
B. Make it short and about exceptions: what moved and is it real, any guardrail near its limit, any experiment needing a decision, and one action with an owner. Owners of normal metrics say “nothing to see here” and move on  ✅ correct
C. Add more metrics, so each team sees its own numbers
D. Replace it with an automated dashboard email and drop the meeting

*Explanation:* The point of a weekly review is noticing change early, not discussion. Amazon's Weekly Business Review is a tactical meeting about exceptions, where an on-track number gets “nothing to see here.” If the review never changes a decision, change the review. Monthly means noticing change later. More metrics makes the ritual longer. An email has nobody to own the variance or agree the action.

*Lesson it draws on:* The Measurement Cadence
